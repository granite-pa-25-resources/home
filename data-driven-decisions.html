📐 Data-Driven Decision Making

Testing the Validity of In-Boundary Enrollment as a School Health Metric

Statistical Analysis of Enrollment Patterns in Area 5

🎯 Executive Summary

The Population Advisory Committee has emphasized in-boundary enrollment rate (% of boundary residents attending their boundary school) as a key metric for school health. This analysis tests whether that metric actually predicts school performance.

❌ Finding: In-boundary enrollment rate shows NO meaningful correlation with actual enrollment performance, projected growth, or enrollment trends.

🧭 Data Note: The in-boundary enrollment rates on the x-axis are calculated from the Mobility Matrices page, using 2024-2025 student mobility data.
The enrollment and trend metrics on the y-axis are drawn from the Enrollment Trends page, covering historical data from 2004-2025. Note that 2025 enrollment data is derived from the 2024-2025 mobility matrices (total students attending each school).
This allows us to assess whether current in-boundary rates are predictive of past and future enrollment outcomes.

📈 Statistical Measures

📖 How to Interpret These Statistics

  • R (Correlation coefficient): Ranges from -1 to +1. Values near 0 indicate no linear relationship. |R| > 0.7 is considered strong correlation.
  • R² (R-squared): Proportion of variance explained. R² < 0.3 means the metric explains less than 30% of variance—too weak to be useful for decisions.
  • RMSE (Root Mean Square Error): Average prediction error in the same units as the metric. Lower is better.
  • MAE (Mean Absolute Error): Average absolute prediction error. More robust to outliers than RMSE.
  • Linear fit line: If in-boundary rate were predictive, points would cluster tightly around the line. Scattered points = poor predictive power.

🔍 Key Findings Across All Metrics

❌ NEGATIVE Correlation with Enrollment Change

Schools with high in-boundary rates (Cottonwood 68.1%, Oakridge 65.2%) show steep declines—Cottonwood declined -34%, Oakridge -51%.

Schools with low in-boundary rates (Morningside 33.7%, Penn 45.1%) showed strong growth (+33%, +30%). No clear pattern—the metric is not predictive!

❌ NEGATIVE Correlation with Current Enrollment

Largest schools (Penn 606, Morningside 561, Oakwood 533) have low to moderate in-boundary rates (45.1%, 33.7%, 30.0%)—far from the highest rates.

Smallest schools (Eastwood 267, Oakridge 277, Rosecrest 291) have varied in-boundary rates (46.8% to 65.2%)—no consistent pattern with school size.

❌ NEGATIVE Correlation with Future Projections

Linear forecasts show schools with high in-boundary rates project declining enrollment, while schools with low in-boundary rates project growth.

High in-boundary rates do NOT predict stable or growing future enrollment—they predict decline.

❌ NEGATIVE Correlation with Capacity Utilization

Schools with higher in-boundary rates tend to have lower capacity utilization (underenrolled buildings).

Schools drawing from across Area 5 (low in-boundary %) have better building utilization. This contradicts the assumption that boundary retention improves efficiency.

💡 Bottom Line

Every performance metric shows either NO correlation or NEGATIVE correlation with in-boundary enrollment rate. This means the PAC's preferred metric is actively misleading. Schools that draw students from across Area 5 (program appeal) consistently outperform schools with high boundary retention.

💡 Policy Recommendations

  1. Stop using in-boundary enrollment rate as a health metric. The data show it has no predictive value for enrollment performance, growth, or sustainability.
  2. Focus on absolute enrollment trends. Is the school growing, stable, or declining over 10+ years? This is what actually matters.
  3. Measure program appeal, not boundary retention. Schools that attract students from across Area 5 demonstrate competitive strength.
  4. Use multiple metrics together:
    • Total enrollment (current size)
    • 21-year enrollment trend (historical trajectory)
    • Linear slope (rate of change)
    • Program diversity (DLI, ALC, specialty tracks)
    • Capacity utilization (% of building capacity)
  5. Recognize that in-boundary rates don't predict school health. Penn (78.9% in-boundary) and Morningside (61.2% in-boundary) are the healthiest schools, with Penn being the ONLY growing school despite having a high in-boundary rate. The relationship is complex and context-dependent, not a simple correlation that can guide closure decisions.

📊 Methodology

Data Sources:

  • In-boundary enrollment rates: Calculated from mobility matrices (2024-25 data)
  • Enrollment data: Granite School District enrollment records (2004-2024)
  • Projections: Linear regression forecasts using 21 years of historical data

Statistical Methods:

  • Pearson correlation coefficient (R) for linear relationships
  • R-squared for explained variance
  • RMSE and MAE for prediction error quantification
  • Ordinary least squares (OLS) linear regression for trend fitting
